Welcome to my /uses page!

Just like other /uses pages this is a catalog of the all the software and hardware that I use as an IT professional.

But mine is a little different from most. It’s quite a bit larger, and more verbose. I’m a bit of a blabber-mouth, and when something excites me I want to gush about it.

So this is more of a catalog of my favorite things (both digital and physical), and way too many words about why I enjoy each of them.

Books

The Metamorphosis of Prime Intellect by Roger Williams

A somewhat obscure bit of sci-fi. It’s essentially a story about the world after the Singularity. It’s most definitely a cautionary tale, meant to demonstrate that being freely given everything that you desire is actively harmful. And that to live a good life requires hard limitations.

I found this by pure chance, most likely on Reddit while I was in college. It has been published on the internet for free by the author, Roger Williams. You can read it on his website here.

Stories of Your Life and Others by Ted Chiang

A very popular short sci-fi anthology. The titular story was the basis for the 2016 movie Arrival. I love this collection, and in general I love Ted Chiang’s writing. He’s able to do more world building in his short stories than other writers can accomplish in years-long series. I describe his work as ‘fictional science’, moresoe than traditional sci-fi. He completely sidesteps all the standard tropes, and it’s so refreshing.

My personal favorite stories are from the very beginning of the book: Tower of Babylon, and Understand.

Manna by Marshall Brain

Another somewhat obscure piece of sci-fi. It’s a story about a near-term future in which development of AI and robotics technology leads to permanent unemployment for most people in (American) society. I first found it years ago, and re-read it regularly.

It’s the first half of the story that I find most interesting. It details a very plausible, step-by-step process that leads ultimately to dystopia. I often measure real technological and social developments that I see against this story, asking myself “How far along the Manna timeline are we now?”. The second half is much more fanciful, about a post-scarcity society which happens to develop in Australia. I find that very uninteresting, so I usually skip it when I’m doing another read.

The story is available for free on Marshall Brain’s personal site. You can read it here.

Everyday Carry

Watch

I wear a Garmin Vivoactive 4. I bought mine used a few years ago and it has worked well for me. I like that it looks very normal, like a traditional watch. The ‘squircle’ shape popularized by Apple doesn’t look right to me. I also really don’t like the ‘bubble’ look of Google’s watches.

It has a decent software ecosystem, with apps and custom watch faces. I played around with the development tools, and even created my own custom watch face. Though I do think Garmin’s Monkey C programming language must have been created by an intern, lol.

The biggest reason I had for getting a Garmin was the transflective LCD display. It reflects light in a way similar to e-ink, which means its fully readable in well lit environments, including direct sunlight. Eventually I think OLED and then MicroLED watches will be bright enough that visibility stops being a concern, but until then I will keep recommending and using Garmin watches.

Games

Jak and Daxter Series

I’m a huge fan of the Jak and Daxter series. I remember very fondly that The Precursor Legacy was the first game I every played on my PS2, all those years ago. I remember being blown away by the graphics, and how fun it was to run, jump and smack around the bad guys.

The original game is a 3D platformer, and ‘collect-a-thon’ style game. The sequels expanded gameplay massively, becoming open worlds with vehicles and gun combat. Some features like skateboarding felt out of place, perhaps added to capitalize on the trends of the time (i.e. the popularity of Tony Hawk games). Regardless, I think every element of these games is very well done.

Jak and Daxter: The Precursor Legacy was the first of the series, released in 2001, followed by Jak II in 2003, and Jak 3 in 2004. I would later pick up Daxter for my PSP in 2006. When I think about this series I marvel at the pace of development in this era. A modern AAA title can take 5 years or more to finish, and within that same timeframe the entire trilogy, as well as a racer and portable spin-off title were released.

I highly recommend this series of games, and you should try it if you haven’t played it (and if you have, play it again!). If you own any recent Playstation console then the Collection is a good way to play. The OpenGoal project is another good option. Though still incomplete, I do believe that eventually it will be the definitive version these games. It is a complete re-implementation of the original game engine. It enables the games to be played on PC at higher resolutions, higher framerates, and it even has mod support. I’ve played the first two games already on my Steam Deck using OpenGoal, and I thought it was amazing.

Update (2026-04-03)

Jak 3 has finally been added to the OpenGoal Launcher! I installed it isntantly on my Steam Deck and so far it works perfectly. The developers are calling this a beta but just as with the Jak II ‘beta’ I don’t expect to see any game-breaking bugs. This has been a long time coming, but I’m so glad that the Jak 3 is finally playable.

So I’ll say now confidently that is the definitive version of the Jak and Daxter Trilogy. If you’ve never played the trilogy, or want to play it again, this is hand’s down the best way to do it.

Monster Hunter Series

Monster Hunter is a fantastic series of games. I’ve been a fan almost as long as the series has existed. I remember going to Blockbuster as a kid and buying Monster Hunter Freedom for my PSP. I’d walk past the box a few times before finally buying it. I was excited to become the hunter on the box, fighting a nasty looking dragon with a sword made from a skull. My favorite game in the series is World, but I also enjoy the newer titles Rise and Wilds.

The premise is straight-forward. You go on hunts in which you either kill or capture a monster. You can carve items from the monster if it was killed, and you always receive additional hunt rewards. Items obtained on the hunts can be used to craft weapons and armor. Hunting increasingly powerful monsters requires better gear, which requires powerful items that must be obtained from hunting certain monsters. This creates a satisfying loop of progression towards the end of the game.

I like to play solo as well as co-op, I highly recommend it.

Tetris

I play a lot of Tetris. I use it primarily as a way to give my mind a break if I’m feeling stressed or fatigued. I’ll also pick it up for a round if I have a bit of time to kill.

There are several Tetris variants that I particularily enjoy:

  • TGM2 - My absolute favorite. I play it nearly every day. I like that it has lock delay and floorkick gameplay mechanics.
  • Gameboy Tetris - My favorite classic Tetris. You can’t argue with 35 million unit sales.
  • Heboris Unofficial Expansion - A fan-made variant. It feels similar to TGM2, and I played it for a long time before discovering TGM2. It’s also open source, and the PS Vita port in particular is very well done.

Mobile Apps

Web Browser

Fennec is 3rd party, community version the official Firefox Mobile app. It is identical to the official app other than removing proprietary code and Mozilla’s data collection. It’s a good but flawed and otherwise inferior to chrome-based mobile browsers in many ways. It doesn’t perform as well, to a degree that you can actually feel. And it is sometimes unable to render pages correctly, when the same page is fine in chrome.

Then why prefer it? Because it respects your privacy more than the alternatives, as well as providing top-shelf ad & tracker blocking through extensions. My favorite extensions from the desktop version are fully supported: uBlock, Privacy Badger, and Decentraleyes.

I care a lot about digital privacy and software freedom, and if you do as well then I strongly recommend Fennec.

Email Client

I use the official Thunderbird mobile app to access email on my phone. It’s very good, the best mobile email app that I’ve used. It was launched in 2024, and is based on an older client called K-9 Mail. I strongly recommend it, especially if you already use Thunderbird on your desktop.

Reddit Client

Red Reader is a mobile client for the social platform called Reddit. Following the API Pricing Controversy in 2023, this is one of the few freely available apps with Reddit’s blessing to continue using the API. It’s a decent app with a simple UI that doesn’t get in your way. It supports anonymous browsing, and has good accessibility features, which is the reason why it was exempted from the new API pricing. If you were looking for a 3rd-party Reddit client, I definitely recommend this one.

Soft Keyboard

I use a custom keyboard on my android phone called Futo. It’s a soft keyboard that has all the modern and advanced features that are available in the first party offering from Google, without any of the privacy concerns.

In particular, I love the voice input. It is done entirely on-device, and you can even change which AI model gets used. For me it has been very accurate, and I’ve come to rely on it for my day-to-day use of my phone.

Futo does nothing new, but it was a revelation for me since I avoided Google and other’s offerings for so long due to privacy concerns. I am concerned no more, and I highly recommend this app.

Phone

My current phone is a Google Pixel 7 Pro. I got it in 2025 and its the first ‘phablet’ style phone I’ve had in years. I enjoy the extra screen real-estate and battery life, but I do sometimes miss the useability of a one-handed phone. The camera is excellent, it’d be weird if it wasn’t. It has a 120hz display, a feature that I can no longer live without. I’m a graphics nerd and pixel peeper, so high refresh rate has always been very significant and obvious to me as a thing to care about. When combined with shortened animations, the feeling of smoothness and snappiness is very satisfying.

Coming from a Samsung Galaxy S20 I was very interested in getting a phone with custom ROM support, which this phone most definitely does (all pixel series phones have it). The S20 is a marvel and an astounding piece of hardware, but it is completely locked down by Samsung, there is no option that I found to even gain root access. That always bothered me, seeing as I’m an absolute control freak with my computers. I might not care to tweak every last detail of my device, but it irritates me greatly if I don’t at least have the choice.

Software

Desktop Operating System

I use Nobara Linux (GNOME Edition) on both my desktop and laptop. It’s an opinionated fork of Fedora, with a focus on gaming and content creation. It has really good defaults and out-of-the-box experience. It works very well for my use case.

Home Server Operating System

OpenMediaVault. This is what I installed on my first home server many years ago, and it is what I use currently. I’ve tried TrueNAS, and even a minimal Debian installation, but I always made my way back to OMV. It provides the core functionality that any NAS would need. It is well designed, and beginner friendly.

IDE for C++

My code editor for c++ projects is Clion. It has the best code completion and linting for c++ that I have ever used, I consider it better than Visual Studio which I regarded as best-in-class for a long time.

I liked it so much I purchased a license but recently a full-featured free version has been released, for non-commercial use. I’m only a little bitter about that.

IDE for Everything else

My go-to code editor for most projects is VSCodium. It’s especially well suited to web devlopment. It’s what I use to develop this site.

It is a fork of Microsoft’s VScode. It’s purpose is to be a drop-in replacement for VScode without Microsoft’s proprietary licensing or telemetry. I care about both of these things which is why I choose to use this over the original.

I try to keep the extensions that I use to a minimum, as they have been demonstrated to be a security risk. I’m pretty amateur as a web developer, so I’ve found CSS Peek to be extremely useful.

Terminal Emulator

Ghostty is a very good terminal emulator, and its currently my favorite. It’s fast, actively maintained, and has a very good default configuration. I like Alacritty as well and did use it for a while, but I switched to Ghostty simply because it integrated better with my GNOME based desktop.

Note Taking

An app called Obsidian. I first discovered it while watching this video by Wendell of Level1Techs. Notes are stored in markdown format text files, and there is a large collection of plugins and themes available for download directly in the user interface.

My note-taking is not as elaborate as Wendell’s, but Obsidian is really good even just as a markdown editor. I have found it useful to be able to create links between my notes, so I do use this feature often. I don’t have much use for the more advanced features like the canvas though. The graph view looks amazing but I don’t find it useful.

It is free to download but I loved it so much I purchased a license. I was really excited when the bases feature entered beta and I wanted to try it before its full release (beta access is limited to the paid version).

Web Browser

Firefox is the best web browser to use if you care about security, privacy, and ad-blocking. I’ve been using it since I was in high school. It was the first browser I ever saw to use tabs, a mind-boggling feature at the time (yes, I’m old).

In recent years Firefox has failed to keep up with its competition. Compared to Chrome it is slower, uses more memory, and crashes more often. Its market share has fallen to just 2.4% in 2025, with a downward trend from the year before.

Despite its flaws I still prefer it for the addon ecosystem. I consider good ad-blocking a requirement for any browser I use, as well as good privacy protections, and with addons Firefox is the only browser that suits my needs.

This is my current list of essential addons:

  • uBlock Origin - The best ad blocker I’ve ever used. Actively maintained.
  • Privacy Badger - Provides additional blocks against online tracking.
  • Decentraleyes - A ‘Local CDN Emulator’, good for both privacy and performance.

Command line Programs

The command utilities that I use. Whether its a vm, container, or bare-metal box I try to have these installed at all times.

  • micro - Text editor. Better than nano.
  • fzf - Easier to use than grep.
  • tldr - Short-form man pages.
  • tmux - For running background terminal sessions.
  • fish - Bash alternative. Good defaults.
  • btop - My preferred system monitor, otherwise very well made.

Tools Updated on Sun May. 10, 2026

Screw Driver Set

This is my main screwdriver kit. I bought it to use while I was working as an IT technician. Whenever I was decommissioning an old computer I’d take the holographic stickers off and put them to the case. It’s a great kit, and I have fond memories for this one.

I paid about $20 on Amazon, from this listing. The brand is Steinwhale, which is a ridiculous name. Everything about this kit from the box to the bits are as cheap as you’d expect, except the driver.

At $20, I feel as though I spent $19.50 on just this driver with the bits and cheap plastic case thrown in as a bonus. It feels hefty, made of solid metal. It’s stubby and comfortable to use one-handed. There are bearings in the top of the handle, which are so smooth that it works well as a fidget toy.

As of writing its $17 CAD on Amazon and if you need a good inexpensive screw driver kit I can’t reccomend this enough.
